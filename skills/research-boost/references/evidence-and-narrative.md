# Evidence and narrative

- Open with a concrete application and the dependency or failure that makes the problem hard. Label imagined examples as illustrative.
- Maintain a source ledger: stable ID, title, authors/group, version/date, official URL, claim, evidence type, figure/table, task, baselines, scale, result and limitation.
- Prefer primary papers, official projects and original figures. Read numeric claims in the actual source. Preserve axes, conditions and uncertainty when cropping.
- Build history around changing constraints and assumptions, not release dates alone. Give each milestone its contribution, remaining limitation and question that follows.
- Map groups to linked contributions. Classify the contribution's openness; a company may publish open research and a university may release no code.
- Use all five frontier criteria from the survey workflow. Record unsupported dimensions honestly. Group reputation is context; it cannot replace measured evidence. Include smaller or new groups when their work covers an important mechanism or setting.
- Name strong baselines and comparable budgets. Avoid a leaderboard assembled from incompatible experiments. State simulation versus hardware, task and tested scale.
- Put an overview before technical deep dives. Explain problem, method, training/construction, evaluation, result and limitation. Put secondary works in a problem-based reading appendix with reasons to read them.
- Derive future questions from observed limitations. For each proposed experiment, name task/data, method, baseline, metric, failure criterion and adoption implication. Label proposals clearly.
- Keep one takeaway per slide and detailed explanation in separate speaker text. Do not imply that a demo link is an embedded or tested video.
